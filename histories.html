History

This section takes a quick look at how animation evolved from simple drawing tricks into a massive cinematic art form. It tracks the major breakthroughs that completely changed how artists create the illusion of life over the decades.

Introduction

Animation has developed through many techniques, from individually drawn pictures to digital characters created using computer software. Understanding how animation techniques work and how they developed helps explain the methods used by animators today.

The Beginnings of Frame-by-Frame Animation

Frame-by-frame animation is one of the fundamental techniques used to create animated movement.

It works by displaying a sequence of still images, with small differences between each image. When the images are shown in quick succession, our eyes perceive movement.

The idea is similar to a flipbook. Each page contains a slightly different drawing, and flipping through the pages creates the illusion that the drawing is moving. This basic principle continues to be used in both traditional hand-drawn animation and modern digital animation.

The Development of Animation Techniques

Although these techniques differ, they all use a sequence of images to create the appearance of movement.

As animation developed, different techniques gave artists new ways to create movement. Traditional hand-drawn animation involved producing individual drawings for successive frames. Stop-motion animation used physical objects or puppets that were moved and photographed one position at a time.

Rotoscoping offered another approach by allowing animators to trace over live-action footage. Later, digital tools made it easier to organise frames, edit movements, create in-between frames, and work with computer-generated characters.

Steamboat Willie and Synchronized Sound

Steamboat Willie was released in 1928 and became famous for its use of synchronized sound with animation. The sound was coordinated with the action on screen, helping create a more immersive viewing experience.

However, it was not the first animated production ever to use synchronized sound. Earlier animated sound experiments and releases existed before it. Its importance comes from the role it played in popularising synchronized sound in animated cartoons, particularly through the success of Mickey Mouse.

The 12 Principles of Animation

These principles became influential guidelines for animators and are still taught and used in animation today.

The 12 principles of animation were developed by Disney animators Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston. They helped explain the techniques animators used to make movement more convincing and expressive. The principles were later presented in their book The Illusion of Life: Disney Animation, published in 1981.

The principles include Squash and Stretch, Anticipation, Staging, Timing, Follow-Through and Overlapping Action, and Exaggeration. Each principle focuses on a different aspect of animation, from making movement feel weighted to ensuring that the audience understands what is happening.

The Move Towards Digital Animation

Computer technology introduced new ways to create and control animated movement. Digital tools allow animators to work with keyframes, create in-betweening, adjust timing, and build characters with 3D rigs.

Rigging is especially useful in 3D animation because a digital skeleton and its controls allow animators to pose and move a character. Instead of drawing every position from scratch, animators can manipulate the character's rig to create a sequence of movements.

Digital animation is now used in films, television, video games, interactive advertisements, and educational tutorials. Traditional techniques remain important, and many modern productions combine different approaches.

Animation Today

Although animation software has changed how many animations are produced, the basic principles remain important. Animators still need to consider timing, spacing, arcs, anticipation, and exaggeration to make movement understandable and engaging.

A traditional animated motion picture commonly plays at 24 frames per second, although different projects can use different frame rates and drawing methods. Whether an animator uses hand-drawn frames, stop-motion photography, or a digital 3D character, the goal is to create movement that communicates an idea or tells a story.
